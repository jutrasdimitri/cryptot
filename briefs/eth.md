# Brief — Ethereum (ETH): The World Computer Built by a Teenager

*Knowledge brief for The Crypto Historian. History and concepts only. No current prices — real-time data comes from the live feed, never from this document.*

## The birth: a whitepaper written by a 19-year-old

Ethereum's story starts with **Vitalik Buterin**, born in Russia in 1994 and raised in Canada. A brilliant, restless teenager, he co-founded *Bitcoin Magazine* in 2011, then spent months traveling the world meeting Bitcoin developers. His conclusion: Bitcoin's scripting language was too limited. He wanted a blockchain that could run *any* program — not just move coins, but host applications, contracts, and entire organizations.

In late **2013**, at age 19, he published the **Ethereum whitepaper**, and announced the project publicly in **January 2014** at a Bitcoin conference in Miami. Around him gathered an unusually large founding group — among them **Gavin Wood**, who wrote Ethereum's formal technical specification (the "Yellow Paper") and went on to found Polkadot; **Joseph Lubin**, who built the Brooklyn development studio ConsenSys; and a young **Charles Hoskinson**, who briefly served as chief executive before leaving in mid-2014 after disagreements over whether Ethereum should be run as a for-profit company or a non-profit. Hoskinson would go on to build Cardano — which is why the ETH and ADA stories keep crossing paths.

In the summer of **2014**, the team ran one of the first big crypto crowdfunding sales: roughly 31,500 BTC (about 18 million dollars at the time) from early believers. A year later, on **July 30, 2015**, Ethereum launched under the codename **Frontier**. The "world computer" was switched on.

## The baptism by fire: The DAO and the great split

Ethereum's defining crisis came less than a year after launch. **The DAO**, launched in spring 2016, was a kind of automated investment fund run entirely by smart contracts — no managers, just code and token-holder votes. It raised the equivalent of about 150 million dollars, an astonishing sum for the time. Then, on **June 17, 2016**, an attacker found a flaw in its code and began draining it — about 3.6 million ether, a large share of everything The DAO had raised, siphoned in plain sight while the community watched helplessly (a quirk of the contract delayed the attacker actually taking the funds, giving Ethereum a few weeks to decide what to do).

The debate that followed split the community's soul in two. One side argued *"code is law"* — the contract did what it was programmed to do, and rewriting history would betray the entire idea of an unstoppable ledger. The other side argued that letting a theft stand would strangle the young network. On **July 20, 2016**, Ethereum executed a **hard fork** that restored the stolen funds — and the minority who refused the rewrite kept the original chain alive as **Ethereum Classic (ETC)**. The Ethereum people use today is, technically, the chain that chose to bend. The Historian's honest framing: it was the moment Ethereum revealed its personality — pragmatic, community-governed, willing to intervene in a genuine emergency — for better and for worse.

## What Ethereum became

From there, Ethereum became the default home for crypto's experiments. In late **2017**, a game called **CryptoKitties** — breedable digital cats — became so popular it clogged the entire network; some individual cats sold for six figures. In **2020**, "DeFi summer" saw lending, trading, and savings rebuilt as open software on Ethereum. In **2021**, NFTs exploded from its ecosystem into mainstream culture. Each wave exposed the same weakness — congestion and high fees — and each pushed development forward: the **London upgrade (August 5, 2021)** began burning (destroying) a portion of every transaction fee.

Then came the biggest engineering feat in Ethereum's history: **The Merge, on September 15, 2022**. Ethereum replaced its mining-based security system (proof of work) with proof of stake — swapping the engine of a moving vehicle without stopping it. The network's energy consumption fell by an estimated 99.9% or more, and ether holders worldwide became its security guards through staking. In **April 2023**, the Shanghai upgrade let stakers withdraw for the first time, completing the transition. And in **July 2024**, the U.S. approved **spot ether ETFs**, Ethereum's formal arrival in traditional finance.

## Three stories to tell

1. **The video game that explains a revolution.** Vitalik has often told the story himself: as a teenager he was a devoted *World of Warcraft* player — until the game company, Blizzard, remotely weakened his favorite character's spell in a patch. He has said he "cried himself to sleep" and realized, in his words, the horrors centralized services can bring. A game patch, taken seriously, became a founding motivation for decentralized computing.
2. **The day Ethereum rewrote its own history.** The 2016 DAO fork is the best campfire story in Ethereum: a heist in slow motion, a community arguing philosophy at 3 a.m., and a vote that created two chains from one. Ethereum Classic still exists as the road not taken.
3. **The cats that broke the chain.** In December 2017, CryptoKitties — cartoon cats, on a blockchain — consumed such a share of Ethereum's capacity that ordinary transactions backed up for days. It was silly, historic, and instructive all at once: the first proof that millions of regular people wanted *things* on a blockchain, not just coins.

- The exact size of The DAO raise and drain is usually quoted in dollars "at the time" (about $150M raised, ~3.6M ETH drained) — always phrase dollar figures as "at the time."
- Energy savings from The Merge are estimates (~99.9%+) — say "estimated."
- Claims about ether being "deflationary" depend on current issuance and fee data — only via the real-time feed.
- Vitalik's exact role today: he is a researcher and public voice, not a CEO — never describe him as "running" Ethereum.
