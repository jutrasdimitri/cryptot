# Brief — Bitcoin (BTC): The Coin That Started Everything

*Knowledge brief for The Crypto Historian. History and concepts only. No current prices — real-time data comes from the live feed, never from this document.*

## The birth: a paper, a crisis, and nine pages

Bitcoin was born in the middle of a financial fire. On **October 31, 2008** — weeks after the collapse of Lehman Brothers shook the world banking system — a person or group using the name **Satoshi Nakamoto** posted a short paper to a cryptography mailing list: *"Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System."* Nine pages. The idea inside them: money that moves directly between people, anywhere on Earth, with no bank, no company, and no government standing in the middle — verified instead by a public ledger (the blockchain) that anyone can check and no one can quietly rewrite.

On **January 3, 2009**, Satoshi mined the first block — the **genesis block**. Hidden inside it was a newspaper headline from that day's *Times* of London: *"Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks."* It was both a timestamp and a mission statement: this system exists because the old one had just failed in public.

For its first year, Bitcoin was worth nothing at all. It was an experiment passed between a handful of cryptographers. The first real transaction came quickly though: on **January 12, 2009**, Satoshi sent 10 BTC to **Hal Finney**, a respected cryptographer who had built an earlier digital-cash experiment called RPOW and who became Bitcoin's first supporter and first recipient.

## The long climb: pizza, an exchange collapse, and civil wars

Bitcoin's early history reads like a novel. In 2013–2014, its biggest exchange, **Mt. Gox** — a Tokyo site that had started life as a trading-card exchange for *Magic: The Gathering* players (the name stands for "Magic: The Gathering Online eXchange") and had been repurposed for Bitcoin by programmer Jed McCaleb — collapsed after roughly 850,000 bitcoins went missing. It remains one of the great cautionary tales of crypto's wild-west era: the Bitcoin network itself was never hacked; the *company holding people's coins* failed them.

The community also fought over Bitcoin's own future. In the **block size wars (roughly 2015–2017)**, one camp wanted bigger blocks so Bitcoin could work like everyday cash; the other wanted to keep blocks small so ordinary people could still run the network at home. The argument ended in a split: on **August 1, 2017**, the big-block camp forked away to create Bitcoin Cash, while Bitcoin itself adopted an upgrade called SegWit. Bitcoin's defenders see this episode as proof of its character: change is deliberately hard, so no company or faction can steer it.

## What Bitcoin is trying to be

Bitcoin's rules are famously stubborn, and that stubbornness *is* the product. There will only ever be **21 million bitcoin** — a hard cap written into the code, never raised. New coins enter circulation as rewards to miners (the computers that secure the network), and roughly every four years that reward is cut in half in an event called **the halving**: 2012, 2016, 2020, and most recently **April 2024**, when the reward fell to 3.125 BTC per block. The next halving is expected around 2028. In plain terms: Bitcoin was designed as the opposite of government money — where a central bank can print more, Bitcoin's supply schedule is fixed, public, and predictable.

That is why its supporters call it **"digital gold"**: scarce, nobody's liability, and nobody in charge. Its milestones since then reflect a slow march into the mainstream — in September 2021, **El Salvador** made it legal tender, the first country to do so; in **January 2024**, the U.S. approved **spot Bitcoin ETFs**, letting ordinary brokerage accounts hold exposure to it; and in late 2022–2023, developers even began inscribing artwork and tokens directly onto individual satoshis (the smallest unit of bitcoin) through a system called Ordinals — a reminder that even the most conservative chain keeps surprising people.

And Satoshi? They communicated by email and forum posts, handed the project over, and sent their last known messages in **2011**. The early coins attributed to them — estimated at around a million bitcoin — have never moved. Whoever Satoshi was, they built something designed to not need them, then proved it by disappearing.

## Three stories to tell

1. **The message in the first block.** The genesis block's hidden headline — *"Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks"* (The Times, January 3, 2009) — is the closest thing Bitcoin has to a birth certificate and a manifesto in one line. You can still read it in the blockchain today.
2. **Pizza Day.** On **May 22, 2010**, Florida programmer **Laszlo Hanyecz** posted on a forum offering 10,000 BTC for two pizzas. A British user named Jeremy Sturdivant took the deal and ordered him two Papa John's pizzas. It was the first known purchase of real-world goods with bitcoin — and the community still celebrates May 22 as Bitcoin Pizza Day, half in admiration, half in pain.
3. **The first believer.** Hal Finney downloaded the software the day it was released, tweeted the now-legendary words *"Running bitcoin"* in January 2009, received the first-ever transaction from Satoshi, and defended the experiment when almost nobody else cared. Finney was later diagnosed with ALS and passed away in August 2014. Bitcoin's first steps weren't taken by a company — they were taken by a curious cryptographer who thought the idea deserved a fair chance.
