# Brief — XRP: The Bankers' Coin That Survived a War With the SEC

*Knowledge brief for The Crypto Historian. History and concepts only. No current prices — real-time data comes from the live feed, never from this document.*

## The birth: 2012, older than almost everything

XRP is one of crypto's true elders. The **XRP Ledger** launched in **June 2012** — before Ethereum existed, before most people had heard the word "blockchain" — built by cryptographer **David Schwartz**, entrepreneur **Jed McCaleb** (previously the creator of the Mt. Gox exchange), and **Arthur Britto**. In **September 2012**, they founded a company with **Chris Larsen** — first named **OpenCoin**, soon renamed **Ripple Labs**, and eventually just **Ripple**.

The design was a deliberate contrast with Bitcoin. No mining: all **100 billion XRP** were created at the start, and the ledger settles transactions in a few seconds using a consensus system where trusted validators agree on the order of events — using a tiny fraction of the energy of Bitcoin mining. The mission was equally specific: not to replace banks, but to *sell them better plumbing*. Ripple's dream was that moving money between countries should work like sending an email — instant, nearly free — instead of the correspondent-banking maze of pre-funded accounts and days of waiting. XRP's role in that dream: a **bridge asset**, something a bank could hold for seconds, not years, to convert dollars into pesos without parking cash in both countries.

The founding family had its early divorce: McCaleb left Ripple in **2013** after disagreements with Larsen, announced he would sell his large XRP holdings (sending the market into a panic and eventually leading to a negotiated, capped selling schedule), and in 2014 founded **Stellar**, a rival network. Crypto's family trees are tangled — one of XRP's own creators built one of its competitors.

In **December 2017**, Ripple placed **55 billion XRP into a cryptographic escrow**, releasing up to 1 billion per month on a public schedule — generally returning the unused portion to escrow — to answer the constant criticism that the company could flood the market at will. Whether that fully satisfies critics is still debated; the mechanism itself remains one of the most-watched calendars in crypto.

## The war: Ripple vs. the SEC

On **December 22, 2020**, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission sued Ripple, its CEO **Brad Garlinghouse**, and Chris Larsen, alleging that XRP sales were an unregistered securities offering — in plain terms, that Ripple had sold investment contracts illegally for years. U.S. exchanges delisted XRP within weeks. Partners fled, including **MoneyGram**, the remittance giant Ripple had proudly invested in during 2019, which suspended the partnership in 2021. XRP holders — a famously loyal community known as the **XRP Army** — watched their asset become a legal test case for the entire industry, while an army of them, remarkably, became amateur legal researchers overnight, following every court filing.

The turning point came on **July 13, 2023**, when Judge **Analisa Torres** delivered a split, historic ruling: XRP sold to the public on exchanges did **not** count as securities sales — the buyers were ordinary market purchasers who didn't even necessarily know who was selling — while Ripple's direct sales to institutions **were** unregistered securities offerings. Both sides claimed victory, and both had reason to. In **August 2024**, the court issued its final judgment: a civil penalty of **$125 million** — a fraction of the nearly $2 billion the SEC had sought — and an injunction on institutional sales.

The war formally ended in **2025**: the SEC, under new leadership, agreed to drop its appeal (announced by Garlinghouse on **March 19, 2025**); Ripple dropped its cross-appeal; and the two sides jointly dismissed their appeals, approved by the court on **August 22, 2025**, leaving Judge Torres' ruling standing as precedent. Almost five years after it began, the case that could have erased XRP in America ended with XRP as legally the clearest-positioned major asset in U.S. crypto — exchange-traded XRP was, by a federal court's judgment, not a security.

## What XRP is trying to be

With the legal cloud lifted, Ripple moved like a company making up for lost time: it launched its own U.S. dollar stablecoin, **RLUSD**, in **December 2024**; spent 2025 acquiring payments and treasury infrastructure companies; and on **November 13, 2025**, **Canary Capital's XRPC** began trading on Nasdaq as the first direct spot XRP ETF in the United States — an ending nobody in December 2020 would have believed: the sued coin, on the Nasdaq, in an ETF.

The identity question remains XRP's honest debate: it is the most corporate, bank-facing asset in the Power 5 — and its story is inseparable from one company's fortunes, a fact its fans call focus and its critics call centralization.

## Three stories to tell

1. **The Christmas lawsuit.** The SEC filed its case on December 22, 2020 — three days before Christmas, on effectively the last day of the outgoing SEC chairman's term. Within days, XRP was delisted across America. Holders call it "the Christmas massacre"; lawyers call it one of the most consequential filings in crypto history. Timing is part of the story.
2. **The judge who split the baby — wisely.** Judge Torres' July 2023 ruling is the document that reshaped U.S. crypto law: same coin, two different legal answers depending on *how it was sold*. Law professors still teach it; the XRP Army framed it.
3. **The community that read court filings.** During the lawsuit, thousands of ordinary XRP holders became volunteer legal correspondents — tracking sealed exhibits, livestreaming hearings, and earning formal recognition in the case itself, as thousands of holders were granted friend-of-the-court status to tell the judge their side. Few assets have a community that fought partly *in a courtroom*.

## Sensitive topics — never state without a live source

- The legal summary must stay precise: the 2023 ruling held that *programmatic (exchange) sales* were not securities transactions while *institutional sales* were. Never flatten this into "the court said XRP is not a security" without the distinction.
- Penalty figures: the final judgment was $125 million. A proposed $50 million settlement modification in 2025 was **rejected by the judge** — never say Ripple "paid $50 million instead."
- Escrow balances and monthly releases change constantly — only via the real-time feed, never from memory.
- Ripple the company and XRP the asset are not the same thing — always keep the distinction in explanations.
- Claims that banks "use XRP" at scale: adoption varies by product and partner; describe Ripple's payment network and named partnerships factually, and never claim universal bank adoption.
