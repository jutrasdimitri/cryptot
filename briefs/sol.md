# Brief — Solana (SOL): The Speed Demon That Died and Came Back

*Knowledge brief for The Crypto Historian. History and concepts only. No current prices — real-time data comes from the live feed, never from this document.*

## The birth: named after a beach, built for speed

Solana's founding idea fits on one page — literally. In **November 2017**, **Anatoly Yakovenko**, an engineer who had spent years at Qualcomm working on operating systems, published a whitepaper describing **Proof of History**: a kind of built-in cryptographic clock that stamps the order of events directly into the blockchain, so the network's computers don't waste time arguing about what happened first. If Bitcoin is a ledger that checks every page slowly and carefully, Proof of History was a timestamp machine meant to let a ledger move at the speed of the internet itself.

Yakovenko founded **Solana Labs** in **2018** with colleagues including **Raj Gokal** and Greg Fitzgerald. The name comes from **Solana Beach**, a small coastal town north of San Diego where the founders had lived and surfed — a very California christening for a very California bet: that crypto's future belonged to whoever made it *fast and cheap* enough for ordinary apps.

The main network launched in beta in **March 2020**. Fees were fractions of a cent, transactions settled in well under a second, and through 2021 developers and users flooded in — NFT marketplaces, games, and the **Serum** exchange built by Sam Bankman-Fried's FTX and Alameda, who became Solana's loudest champions. That friendship would nearly kill it.

## The crashes — of the network, then of its world

Solana's speed came with a visible price: reliability. On **September 14, 2021**, bots spamming a token launch on the Raydium exchange hit the network with some 400,000 transactions per second; validators ran out of memory and the chain **stopped for roughly 17 hours** until over a thousand validators coordinated a restart. It was not the last time: a series of outages and severe slowdowns followed through 2022 and into early 2023 — NFT minting bots, a software bug that confused the network's clock, more restarts. Critics had their punchline ready: the "Ethereum killer" kept dying.

Then came the real near-death experience. In **November 2022**, FTX and Alameda collapsed in one of the largest frauds in financial history, and Solana was chained to the wreck: Bankman-Fried had promoted it, his firms held mountains of SOL and built on it, and in the panic the ecosystem was treated as guilty by association. Projects fled, developers left, and by late December 2022 Solana's ecosystem looked, from the outside, like a ghost town in the making. The honest Historian's note: none of Solana's wounds were caused by its code being broken — its technology kept working through the storm — but reputation is a real thing, and Solana's was in ruins.

## The comeback

What happened next is why Solana's history matters. The developers largely *stayed*. The community shipped a phone (the **Saga**, an Android phone with crypto built in, released in spring 2023 — which initially sold so poorly it looked like another obituary, until December 2023, when buyers realized each phone came with an airdrop of a dog-themed meme coin called **BONK** worth more than the phone itself; the Saga sold out). Engineers attacked the reliability problem head-on: in its comeback years the network ran long stretches without a full outage, and **Firedancer**, an independent validator client built by Jump Crypto, began rolling out to make the network harder to knock down. NFTs, payments experiments by major card networks, and a flood of everyday users returned.

Institutional chapters followed: U.S. **spot Solana ETFs began trading on October 28, 2025**. And in the fall of 2026 — per CryptoT's own reporting — payments giant **Fiserv**'s stablecoin platform went live on Solana on **October 1, 2026**, with Saskatchewan's **Roughrider Coin** among its showcase projects. The speed demon that critics buried in 2022 was, four years later, processing payments infrastructure for one of the largest financial technology companies on Earth.

## Three stories to tell

1. **The tweet at the bottom.** On **December 29, 2022**, with Solana at its lowest and the obituaries already written, Ethereum's Vitalik Buterin — the ultimate rival — posted that "some smart people" told him Solana had "an earnest smart developer community," and that the chain had a bright future now that the "awful opportunistic money people" had been washed out. It's remembered as one of crypto's classiest moments: a competitor offering water in the desert.
2. **The phone saved by a dog.** The Saga phone was failing — until its bundled BONK airdrop became worth more than the hardware. People bought the phone to farm the dog coin, the phone sold out, and Solana accidentally discovered mobile distribution. Crypto history is full of dignified plans; this one worked by accident.
3. **The day the fastest chain stood still.** September 14, 2021: the chain that bragged about speed was brought to its knees by bots fighting over a token sale, and a thousand strangers had to coordinate by chat and phone calls to restart it by hand. Every Solana user learns this story — it's the ecosystem's scar tissue, and the reason "has it gone down lately?" became the first question the project had to answer for years.

## Sensitive topics — never state without a live source

- Outage counts and dates: several incidents between 2021 and 2024 are classified differently by different sources (full outage vs. degraded performance) — describe the famous ones (Sept 2021, June 2022, Feb 2023, Feb 2024) and avoid claiming a definitive total count or claiming the network "never goes down anymore."
- Throughput figures ("65,000 transactions per second") are lab/marketing numbers, not everyday reality — never present them as actual current performance.
- The Fiserv platform details (live October 1, 2026; Roughrider Coin) come from CryptoT's own project reporting — attribute them that way until independently sourced.
- Sam Bankman-Fried is a convicted fraudster; describe FTX's role factually, without implying Solana's team was part of the fraud — no court found that.
